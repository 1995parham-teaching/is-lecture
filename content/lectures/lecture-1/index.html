What Are We Protecting?

Before any mechanism, name the property. Almost every requirement in this course is one of three.

Confidentiality Only those authorized can read it
Integrity Only those authorized can change it — and you can tell
Availability Those authorized can actually use it, when they need to

The CIA triad. Every control we meet serves one of them.

The Same System, Three Failures

A university grade database:

  • Confidentiality — a student reads everyone else's grades
  • Integrity — a student changes their own grade from 12 to 20
  • Availability — nobody can submit grades on the day of the deadline

Which is worst? It depends on the system — and deciding that is called risk management (lecture 2).

Three Is Not Always Enough

  • Authenticity — this really did come from who it claims to
  • Non-repudiation — and the sender cannot later deny it
    • Needs a digital signature, not just a MAC (lecture 3)
  • Accountability — every action traces back to a responsible identity
  • Privacy — the subject keeps control over data about them, which is not the same as confidentiality

FIPS 199 defines confidentiality, integrity and availability; authenticity and accountability are added by NIST SP 800-33.

Integrity, in One Command


$ cat order.txt
transfer 100 USD to alice

$ shasum -a 256 order.txt
918e1e440fd83a713e79f2bcfcdecedb1145a7e94dba18ae71c9a9f886a7bb26  order.txt

$ sed -i '' s/100/900/ order.txt      # change one character

$ shasum -a 256 order.txt
73d418b01e29a3ec522895c90d805794864d516a5603644e426adbd21a3111d9  order.txt

One digit moved, and every digit of the digest moved with it. That is what "you can tell" means.

But Who Guards the Digest?

  • An attacker who can rewrite the file can rewrite the digest next to it
  • A hash gives integrity against accident; it takes a key to get integrity against an adversary
  • This pattern repeats all term: a mechanism only moves the problem, and the question is where it moved to

Security is not a product, but a process. — Bruce Schneier, 2000

Say It Precisely

These four words are used interchangeably in the press. Not here.

Asset Something with value: data, a service, a reputation
Vulnerability A weakness in the asset or its controls
Threat A potential cause of harm — needs an actor and a motive
Attack A threat that someone actually carried out
Risk How likely, and how bad — the number we manage

A vulnerability with no threat is a curiosity. A threat with no vulnerability is noise.

Passive and Active

The oldest useful split, and it is the one X.800 uses:

  • Passive — the attacker only observes. Nothing changes, so there is nothing to notice
    • Release of message contents · traffic analysis
    • You cannot detect it; you prevent it, with encryption
  • Active — the attacker modifies the stream or fakes one
    • Masquerade · replay · modification · denial of service
    • You cannot prevent all of it; you detect it and recover

Traffic Analysis Is Not Nothing

  • Encryption hides what was said. It does not hide that it was said
  • Who talked to whom, when, how often, how much — the metadata
  • A phone that contacts an oncology clinic weekly has leaked something, and every byte was encrypted
  • Countermeasures are expensive: traffic padding, cover traffic, mixing. This is why they are rare

Who Is the Attacker?

"A hacker" is not a threat model. The defence depends entirely on the budget on the other side.

Opportunist Automated scanning. Takes whatever is unpatched and reachable
Insider Already authenticated, already inside. Hardest to stop
Organized crime Ransomware, fraud. Follows the money and is patient
State actor Buys zero-days, targets supply chains, will not give up

Defending against all four to the same standard is how budgets are wasted.

Attack Surface

  • Every place untrusted input can reach your system: open ports, HTTP parameters, file uploads, USB slots, help desk staff
  • The first and cheapest defence is always to make it smaller
    • Turn the service off · close the port · remove the feature
    • Code that is not there has no vulnerabilities
  • Attack tree: put the goal at the root, the ways to reach it beneath, and keep asking "how?" until the leaves are things a person could actually do

STRIDE

A checklist for "what could go wrong here", one letter per property:

Spoofing pretending to be someone → authenticity
Tampering changing data → integrity
Repudiation denying you did it → non-repudiation
Information disclosure reading what you should not → confidentiality
Denial of service taking it away → availability
Elevation of privilege becoming admin → authorization

Walk a data-flow diagram and ask all six at every boundary.

No Single Wall

  • Every control fails eventually — it is misconfigured, out of date, or simply bypassed
  • Defence in depth: arrange controls so that no single failure is fatal
  • The castle analogy is popular and slightly wrong: a moat only helps if the attacker comes by the gate. Most arrive by email

Assume the outer layer is already broken. What does the next one do?

The Layers

Physical Locks, racks, guards. Console access beats every other control
Network Zoning, firewalls, VPN, segmentation — lecture 7
Host Hardening, patching, anti-malware, host IDS — lecture 5
Application Input handling, authentication, session management — lecture 6
Data Encryption at rest and in transit, key management — lecture 3
People Training, policy, process. The layer that is always attacked

Four Kinds of Mechanism

Whatever the layer, a control does one of four jobs. A programme with only the first is not a programme.

  • Prevent — stop it happening. Access control, encryption, input validation
  • Detect — notice that it happened. Logs, IDS, file-integrity monitoring
  • Respond — contain it. Isolate the host, revoke the key, block the address
  • Recover — get back to normal. Backups, failover, the rebuild you have actually rehearsed

Prevention is preferred, detection is mandatory, recovery is survival.

Principles Worth Memorizing

  • Least privilege — every subject gets the rights it needs, and no more
  • Fail safe defaults — deny unless explicitly allowed
  • Economy of mechanism — a simple design is one you can actually check
  • Complete mediation — check every access, not just the first
  • Open design — security must not depend on the design being secret
  • Separation of privilege — two keys are better than one
  • Least common mechanism · psychological acceptability

Saltzer and Schroeder, 1975. They have not needed revising.

The Awkward Truths

  • The attacker only has to be right once. You have to be right every time — but you also get to watch, and they do not
  • Security is a trade-off. A control that makes the job impossible will be worked around, and then you have neither security nor the control
  • Complexity is the enemy. Every feature, dependency and integration is more surface
  • There is no perfect. The goal is to make the attack cost more than the asset is worth
