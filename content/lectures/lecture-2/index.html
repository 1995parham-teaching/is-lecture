Why a Standard Vocabulary?

  • Lecture 1 gave us words. Two engineers can still mean different things by "the channel is authenticated"
  • ITU-T X.800 — the Security Architecture for OSI, published 1991, identical in substance to ISO 7498-2
  • Old, and still the backbone of how requirements get written down: a requirement names a service, a design names a mechanism

The value is not the document. It is that everybody uses the same six nouns.

Three Definitions

Security attack Any action that compromises the security of information
Security mechanism A process designed to detect, prevent or recover from an attack
Security service A capability that counters attacks, built from one or more mechanisms
  • A service is what you promise; a mechanism is how you deliver it
  • "Confidentiality" is a service. "AES-256-GCM" is a mechanism. Never write the second where the first belongs

The Five Service Categories

Authentication The communicating party is who it claims — peer entity, and data origin
Access control Prevent unauthorized use of a resource — lecture 4
Data confidentiality Protect from disclosure — including the traffic-flow variety
Data integrity The data is as sent, with or without recovery
Non-repudiation Of origin, or of delivery — neither side can deny it happened

Availability is treated as a property of the system in X.800 rather than a service of its own — the one place the standard shows its age.

Specific Mechanisms

Eight, each sitting at a particular protocol layer:

  • Encipherment — the maths of lecture 3
  • Digital signature — unforgeable, and verifiable by anyone
  • Access control — enforcing rights
  • Data integrity — detecting modification
  • Authentication exchange — a handshake that proves identity
  • Traffic padding — filler, so the volume says nothing
  • Routing control — choose a path you trust
  • Notarization — a third party vouches for the exchange

Pervasive Mechanisms

Five more that belong to no layer, because they apply to the whole system:

  • Trusted functionality — the part that is correct by assumption, so keep it small
  • Security labels — the resource carries its own sensitivity
  • Event detection — noticing that something security-relevant happened
  • Security audit trail — the record you read afterwards
  • Security recovery — what the system does in response

The last three are lecture 1's detect · respond · recover, thirty years earlier.

Read the bottom row: non-repudiation is the one service encipherment cannot provide. Only a signature binds a message to a single party.

From Vocabulary to Architecture

  • X.800 tells you what to call things. An enterprise security architecture says where they go in your organization
  • It answers, on one page each:
    • What are the assets, and who owns each one?
    • What are the zones, and what crosses between them?
    • Which control enforces which requirement, and who operates it?
  • Without it you get point solutions: nine products, six of them overlapping, and one gap nobody owns

Layers of an Architecture

SABSA's six views — each one asks the same questions of a new audience:

Contextual The business. What are we in the business of losing?
Conceptual The architect. Domains, trust, principles
Logical The designer. Services, policies, information flows
Physical The builder. Mechanisms, protocols, data structures
Component The tradesman. Products, versions, standards
Operational The manager. Who runs it, and how it is measured

Requirements flow down. Assurance flows back up.

Zones and Boundaries

  • A zone is a set of assets with the same trust level and the same policy
  • The interesting security is never inside a zone. It is at the boundary
    • Every boundary needs one place traffic must pass through
    • Every boundary is where you authenticate, filter and log
  • Draw them once and the firewall rules, the network design and the audit scope all fall out of the same picture — lecture 7 builds this properly

You Cannot Protect What You Cannot List

  • Asset inventory is unglamorous and it is the control that fails first
  • Every breach report has the same sentence in it somewhere: "the server was not in the inventory"
  • Each asset needs an owner, a classification, and a location
    • Classification — public · internal · confidential · restricted
    • The label decides which controls apply, so it must be a short list
  • CIS Control 1 and 2 are inventory of hardware and software. They are first on purpose

The Standards You Will Meet

ISO/IEC 27001 The requirements for an ISMS. What you get certified against
ISO/IEC 27002 The control catalogue — guidance, not requirements
NIST CSF Govern · Identify · Protect · Detect · Respond · Recover
NIST SP 800-53 A very large control catalogue, US federal in origin
CIS Controls 18 controls in priority order. The best place to start
PCI DSS Card data. Not optional if you touch it

They overlap heavily. Pick one as the spine and map the others onto it.

Four Documents, Often Confused

Policy What must be true, and who says so. Short. Signed by management
Standard The specific, mandatory way — "TLS 1.2 or above"
Procedure The steps, in order, for a human to follow
Guideline Advice. The only one that is optional
  • A policy that names a product is really a standard, and it will be obsolete before the next review

What a Policy Has to Contain

  • Scope — who and what it binds
  • Statement — the requirement, in the imperative
  • Roles — who owns it, who enforces it, who may grant an exception
  • Consequences — what happens when it is broken
  • Review date — a policy with no review date is a historical document

If nothing in the organization would change when the policy is withdrawn, it was never a policy.

The ISMS Loop

  • An Information Security Management System is the machinery that keeps policy connected to reality
  • Plan · Do · Check · Act, forever
    • Plan — assess risk, choose controls
    • Do — implement and operate them
    • Check — measure, audit, review incidents
    • Act — fix what the check found
  • ISO 27001 certifies the loop, not the controls. An organization can be certified and still be badly defended

Why Policies Fail

  • Nobody read it. Forty pages, once, at onboarding
  • It forbids the job. If the rule blocks the work, the work wins — every time
  • No exception process. Without a legitimate way to say "not here", people take the illegitimate one
  • Never measured. An unaudited policy describes intentions, not systems

The best policy is the one that is enforced by a mechanism, so following it is the path of least resistance.

You Cannot Fix Everything

  • Budgets are finite and vulnerabilities are not. Risk management is the discipline of deciding what not to fix
  • Risk = likelihood × impact, on the asset, given the threat and the vulnerability
  • The loop, from ISO 27005 and NIST SP 800-30:
    1. Identify — assets, threats, vulnerabilities
    2. Analyse — how likely, how bad
    3. Evaluate — compare against the appetite
    4. Treat — do something about it
    5. Monitor — because all four inputs move

Qualitative or Quantitative?

  • Qualitative — high / medium / low on a 5×5 matrix
    • Fast, needs no data, and everyone can join in
    • "High" is not a number — you cannot add two of them, or compare across teams
  • Quantitative — money per year
    • Comparable, and speaks the language of the budget holder
    • Needs frequency data that most organizations simply do not have
  • In practice: qualitative to triage, quantitative for the few decisions big enough to deserve it

Putting a Number On It

  • SLE — single loss expectancy = asset value × exposure factor
  • ARO — annualized rate of occurrence, how many times a year
  • ALE — annualized loss expectancy = SLE × ARO

The control is worth buying when it costs less than the ALE it removes. That is the entire argument, and it can be written down.

The Control That Does Not Pay


asset_value  = 400_000      # the customer database, if it leaks
exposure     = 0.35         # fraction of that value one breach destroys
frequency    = 0.40         # breaches per year, from history

sle = asset_value * exposure
ale = sle * frequency
    

SLE = 400,000 x 0.35      =      140,000
ALE = 140,000 x 0.4       =       56,000 per year

So Do We Buy It?


ALE after control              =       14,000 per year
value of the control          =       42,000 per year
cost of the control           =       60,000 per year
net                           =      -18,000 per year
  • A control that works exactly as advertised, and it still loses money
  • Now the argument is about the inputs — is the database really worth 400,000? — which is the argument worth having

Four Things To Do With a Risk

Avoid Stop doing the activity. Do not collect the data at all
Reduce Apply a control. The one everyone thinks of first
Transfer Insurance, or a contract. Moves the cost, never the duty
Accept Decide it is tolerable — in writing, with a named owner
  • What is left after treatment is residual risk, and someone senior has to sign for it
  • Ignoring a risk is not accepting it. Accepting it has a signature on it

What the Numbers Cannot Do

  • Frequency for a rare, deliberate event is not a frequency you can measure. An adversary is not weather
  • Reputational and human impact resist pricing, and get left out of the model precisely because they resist pricing
  • The model is a way to make assumptions explicit and arguable — not an oracle

All models are wrong, but some are useful. — George Box

When It Happens Anyway

  • Event — anything observable · Alert — an event worth a look · Incident — an event that actually harmed the properties
  • The four phases of NIST SP 800-61, and note that the middle one is a loop
    1. Preparation
    2. Detection and analysis
    3. Containment, eradication and recovery
    4. Post-incident activity
  • Phase 1 is the only one you can do today, and it decides how the other three go

Preparation Is the Whole Game

  • A named team, reachable out of hours and out of band — if the corporate mail is compromised, do not run the response on corporate mail
  • Logs that already exist, centralized, with a retention long enough to cover the gap between breach and discovery
  • Decisions made in advance: who may disconnect production, who talks to the press, when law enforcement is called
  • Rehearsed. A tabletop exercise finds the missing phone number before the night you need it

Containment Is a Trade-Off

  • Pull the plug and you stop the damage — and destroy the evidence, and tell the attacker they are seen
  • Watch and learn and you keep the evidence — and the damage keeps running
  • Short-term containment stops the bleeding; long-term containment is the clean rebuild
  • Eradication means the cause is gone, not the symptom. Restoring a backup onto the same unpatched host is not eradication
  • Preserve volatile evidence first: memory, then disk, then logs

Afterwards

  • A post-incident review within two weeks, while people still remember
  • Blameless. The question is what let a mistake become an incident, not who made it
    • Blame produces silence, and silence is how the next one goes undetected
  • Output is a small number of dated actions with owners
  • Feed it back into the risk register — an incident is measured frequency data, the thing the model was missing

Business Continuity

  • Incident response saves the system. Business continuity keeps the organization running while the system is gone
  • It starts with a Business Impact Analysis: which processes matter, and how long can each be down
  • Two numbers per process, and they are not the same number
    • RTO — recovery time objective: how long until it is back
    • RPO — recovery point objective: how much data you can afford to lose
  • An RPO of one hour means backups every hour. The number is the requirement

Backups, Honestly

  • 3-2-1 — three copies, on two kinds of media, one of them off-site
  • At least one copy offline or immutable — ransomware looks for the backup server first, and it is usually reachable with the same credentials
  • An untested backup is not a backup. Restore one on a schedule, and time it — that measured time is your real RTO
  • Encrypt them. A backup tape is a copy of everything, in a box, in a van

The Same Code, Two Mechanisms

Lecture 1's fix, read with this lecture's eyes:


	if roles[user] != "instructor" {              // access control
		log.Printf("DENIED grade %s=%d by %s", id, grade, user)
		http.Error(w, "forbidden", http.StatusForbidden)
		return
	}
	grades[id] = grade
	log.Printf("grade %s=%d by %s", id, grade, user)  // security audit trail
  • The if is a specific mechanism: access control, at one layer, in one place
  • The log is a pervasive one: the audit trail, which belongs to no layer and watches every request
  • Together they deliver the data integrity service. Neither does on its own: the check stops the change, the log proves what happened
  • Nothing here helps the reader on the lab wifi. That service is confidentiality, its mechanism is encipherment, and it is lecture 3

The Policy Is a Test

"Only the instructor of record may enter or change a grade." Written as a test, it runs on every commit: 


func TestOnlyInstructorSetsGrade(t *testing.T) {
	req := httptest.NewRequest("POST", "/grade?id=4001&grade=20", nil)
	req.Header.Set("X-User", "4001") // a student
	rec := httptest.NewRecorder()
	setGrade(rec, req)
	if rec.Code != http.StatusForbidden {
		t.Fatalf("a student changed a grade: HTTP %d", rec.Code)
	}
}

Is the Check Worth It?

Lecture 1 asked which failure is worst. Priced like the customer database: 


check_cost = 1_000          # one developer-day, paid once
sle        = 5_000          # one hearing, one reissued transcript
aro        = 2              # every term, someone tries

ale = sle * aro
    

ALE               = 5,000 x 2  =   10,000 per year
check pays back in  36 days
  • Reduce: a day of work against 10,000 a year. The check is bought
  • The deadline-day outage costs one extended deadline a year. A second server costs more than that. Accept, in writing, with a name on it

The Day It Happens


$ grep -v 'by parham' server.log
2026/10/06 01:01:23 grade 4001=20 by 4001
  • Alert: the nightly grep finds a grade changed by someone who is not the instructor
  • Incident: the instructor confirms it was not them. Freeze grade entry, copy the log before touching anything
  • Eradicate: deploy the check. The test is green. Recover: the log says 12, so 12 goes back
  • Afterwards: the test now exists, so the check cannot go missing again. And "twice a year" in the risk register becomes a count of one, with a date

One program, two lectures. Every idea had a line of code.

Where This Leaves Us

  • We can now name a requirement as a service, write it as policy, justify it with risk, and plan for the day it fails
  • Every one of those services eventually rests on the same small set of mechanisms — and most of those are cryptography
