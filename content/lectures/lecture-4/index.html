The Most-Used Service

  • Of X.800's five services, this is the one every system implements, and the one most often implemented badly
  • The question is always the same shape: may this subject perform this action on this object?
  • Subject — a user, a process, a service. Object — a file, a row, an endpoint

Everything in this lecture is one function: allow(s, a, o)

The Four A's

Authentication Are you who you claim to be?
Authorization Given that, are you allowed to do this?
Accounting What did you consume, and for how long?
Auditing What did you do, recorded so it can be reviewed
  • RADIUS and Diameter ship the first three as AAA; the fourth is added when the record has to survive a dispute
  • Some texts split identification off from authentication instead — claiming a name is not the same as proving it

Identify, Then Authenticate, Then Authorize

  • Identification — "I am parham". Public, and not a claim of anything
  • Authentication — evidence for that claim
  • Authorization — the rights that identity carries

They fail differently, and confusing them is a real bug class. An authenticated user who reaches another user's records has an authorization failure — the login worked perfectly.

That bug has a name — broken access control — and it has been number one in the OWASP Top Ten since 2021. Lecture 6 returns to it.

Audit Is Not Optional

  • Prevention fails. The log is what turns a breach into a known breach
  • Record the decision, not just the action: who, what, when, and allowed or denied
  • Denied attempts are the interesting ones. A thousand denials from one account is the alert
  • Send logs somewhere the subject cannot edit — an attacker with root edits the local log first
  • Do not log the secret. Passwords, tokens and card numbers in a log file are a breach waiting for a log reader

The Access Matrix

The abstract model: subjects down the side, objects across the top.

grades.db report.pdf /etc/shadow
parham read, write read —
student — read —
root read, write read, write read, write

Nobody stores it as a matrix — it is enormous and mostly empty. You store it by column or by row, and that choice has consequences.

Columns or Rows

  • Access control list — store a column, attached to the object
    • "Who may touch this file?" is instant
    • "What may this user touch?" means walking every object in the system
    • Unix permissions, Windows ACLs, S3 bucket policies
  • Capability — store a row, held by the subject as an unforgeable ticket
    • Fast to check, easy to delegate — and hard to revoke
    • A bearer token is a capability, which is exactly why lecture 6 worries about where it is stored

DAC — the Owner Decides

  • Discretionary access control: whoever owns an object sets its permissions
  • Flexible, familiar, and the default nearly everywhere
  • Two weaknesses, both structural:
    • One careless owner can expose anything they own — chmod 777 is one keystroke
    • Rights are transitive. A program you run acts with your rights, so any program you run can give your data away

DAC on This Machine


$ ls -l salaries.txt
-rw-r--r--  1 parham  wheel  7 Aug 18 00:51 salaries.txt

$ chmod 640 salaries.txt && ls -l salaries.txt
-rw-r-----  1 parham  wheel  7 Aug 18 00:51 salaries.txt

$ chmod u=rw,g=r,o= salaries.txt      # the same thing, spelled out

$ umask
022

umask 022 is why the file was world-readable when it was created. The default is permissive, and defaults are what most files get.

Two Bits Worth Knowing


$ ls -l /usr/bin/su
-rwsr-xr-x  1 root  wheel  121904 Jun 25 02:29 /usr/bin/su

$ ls -ld /private/tmp
drwxrwxrwt  110 root  wheel  3520 Aug 18 00:51 /private/tmp
  • s — setuid. The program runs as its owner, not as you. That is how an ordinary user changes a password in a root-owned file
  • t — sticky. Everyone may write in /tmp, but only the owner of a file may delete it
  • Every setuid binary is a privilege boundary — a bug in one is a local root exploit

MAC — the System Decides

  • Mandatory access control: a system-wide policy overrides the owner. You cannot share what the policy forbids, even if you want to
  • Every subject has a clearance, every object a label, and the rules compare them
  • Bell–LaPadula protects confidentiality
    • No read up — you cannot read above your clearance
    • No write down — and you cannot copy it downward either
  • Biba protects integrity, and is exactly upside down: no read down, no write up
  • SELinux, AppArmor, iOS and Android sandboxes are all MAC

RBAC — the Role Decides

  • Rights attach to roles; users are assigned roles. Nobody is granted a permission directly
  • It matches how organizations actually work, which is why it won: teaching assistant is a job, and the job has a fixed set of rights
  • When someone changes job you change one assignment, not forty permissions
  • Two refinements that matter in practice:
    • Role hierarchy — professor inherits everything a lecturer may do
    • Separation of duty — no one person may hold both request payment and approve payment

ABAC — the Context Decides

  • RBAC cannot express "during office hours", "from a managed device", "for their own department" without inventing a role per combination — role explosion
  • Attribute-based access control evaluates a rule over attributes of the subject, the object, the action and the environment
  • Very expressive, and much harder to answer the auditor's question: "who can read this today?"
  • Most real systems are RBAC with a few attributes bolted on, and that is a reasonable place to land

Choosing

DAC Owner sets it Flexible Leaks by accident
MAC Policy sets it Strong, uniform Rigid, heavy to administer
RBAC Role sets it Matches the org chart Role explosion
ABAC Rules over attributes Expressive, contextual Hard to audit

Whichever you pick, the two principles from lecture 1 still decide the outcome: least privilege and deny by default.

Three Kinds of Evidence

Something you know Password, PIN, the answer to a question
Something you have A phone, a security key, a smart card
Something you are Fingerprint, face, iris, the way you type
  • Multi-factor means factors from different rows. A password plus a security question is one factor asked twice
  • MFA is the single highest-value control on this slide, because it breaks the attack that scales: stolen passwords

Something You Have

  • OTP over SMS — better than nothing, and the weakest of these. SIM swap, SS7 interception, and the code is phishable
  • TOTP — an authenticator app. A shared secret plus the clock, so it works offline
  • FIDO2 / WebAuthn — a key pair per site, held in hardware
    • The one design that is phishing-resistant: the browser signs over the real origin, so a look-alike domain gets a signature that does not verify
    • Nothing shared, nothing to type, nothing to leak from the server

TOTP Is Twenty Lines


counter := make([]byte, 8)

mac := hmac.New(sha1.New, secret)
mac.Write(counter)
sum := mac.Sum(nil)

offset := sum[len(sum)-1] & 0x0f            // dynamic truncation
code := binary.BigEndian.Uint32(sum[offset:offset+4]) & 0x7fffffff
    

T=59           code=94287082

Those are the RFC 6238 test vectors, reproduced exactly.

What That Code Tells You

  • It is an HMAC of the current time, cut down to six or eight digits. No magic
  • The server needs the same secret — so a server breach hands over everyone's second factor
  • Both sides need the same clock. That is why a drifting phone stops working, and why servers accept a window of one or two steps
  • And it is phishable: a fake site asks for the six digits and forwards them within thirty seconds. WebAuthn exists because of this

Passwords Are Still Here

  • Cheap, universal, need no hardware, and everyone already knows how they work
  • And they are reused, guessable, phishable, and shared over chat
  • Two problems that need separating:
    • How the user chooses one — a human-factors problem
    • How you store it — an engineering problem, and entirely your fault when it goes wrong

Never Store It Twice the Same


$ printf 'hunter2' | shasum -a 256
f52fbd32b2b3b86ff88ef6c490628285f482af15ddcb29541f94bcf526a3f6c7
$ printf 'hunter2' | shasum -a 256
f52fbd32b2b3b86ff88ef6c490628285f482af15ddcb29541f94bcf526a3f6c7

$ openssl passwd -6 hunter2
$6$MrTqQCcVCjfH/jFG$rM65BZ4dxysbt8.J82C6D8e5IW8sC.3Ufq...
$ openssl passwd -6 hunter2
$6$9kSX7tN9L2s6M5N.$g.pyl63tv1ad97rS8/Il3yZSEaZ34j25vs...
  • Unsalted: the same digest, on every machine, forever. Precompute it once and it is broken everywhere — a rainbow table
  • Salted: a random value per password, stored beside it. Two identical passwords now look nothing alike, and every attack must be redone per user

Salt Is Not Enough

A salt stops precomputation. It does nothing about speed — and speed is what an offline attacker has. 


sha256(password)                      0.000 ms/hash    3,682,588 guesses/sec
pbkdf2-hmac-sha256, 600k iter        57.918 ms/hash           17 guesses/sec
scrypt, N=2^17 r=8 p=1              198.368 ms/hash            5 guesses/sec
  • On one laptop core. A password hashed with SHA-256 falls 200,000 times faster than the same password behind scrypt
  • A password hash is supposed to be slow. It is the only place in this course where slow is the requirement

What To Actually Use

  • Argon2id — the current recommendation. Tunable in time, memory and parallelism
  • scrypt · bcrypt — both fine, both still widely deployed
  • PBKDF2 — acceptable, and often the only option in a certified environment
  • Never a bare MD5, SHA-1 or SHA-256
  • Memory-hard is the point of the modern ones: GPUs have thousands of cores and not much memory per core, so requiring memory takes the attacker's advantage away
  • Tune the cost so verification takes a few hundred milliseconds on your hardware, and revisit it as hardware improves

Attacks on Passwords

Online guessing Against your login form. Rate limit, lock out, add delay
Offline cracking Against a stolen database. Only the KDF slows it down
Credential stuffing Reusing another site's breach. Nothing you do to your own hashing helps
Password spraying One common password against every account, to stay under the lockout
Phishing The user types it in for you. Beats every storage decision

Note that only two of the five are affected by how you hash. The other three need MFA.

The Rules Changed

NIST SP 800-63B says most of the old advice was counterproductive:

  • No forced periodic rotation — it produces Summer2026! then Autumn2026!. Rotate on evidence of compromise
  • No composition rules — demanding a symbol and a digit adds almost no entropy and a great deal of annoyance
  • Length over complexity — allow long passphrases, and allow every character including spaces
  • Check against known-breached lists — this is the change that actually removes weak passwords
  • Allow paste, so password managers work. Blocking it drives people to worse passwords

Something You Are

  • Fingerprint · face · iris · retina · voice · hand geometry · gait · keystroke dynamics
  • Two phases: enrollment builds a template, then each use compares a fresh sample against it
  • The comparison is never exact. Your finger is never presented twice the same way, so the system computes a similarity score and compares it to a threshold
  • That threshold is the whole design

FAR, FRR and the Threshold

  • FAR — false acceptance rate. An impostor is let in
  • FRR — false rejection rate. The right person is turned away
  • One knob moves both, in opposite directions. Tighten the threshold and FAR falls while FRR rises
  • EER — the crossover, where the two are equal. A single number for comparing systems, and rarely the setting you want
  • Pick by consequence: a phone unlock tolerates FAR to keep FRR low; a vault does the opposite
  • A high FRR has a security cost too — it is what makes staff prop the door open

The Two Facts People Forget

A biometric is a username, not a password.

  • It is not secret. You leave fingerprints on every glass and your face is on every camera
  • It cannot be revoked. A leaked password is changed in a minute. A leaked fingerprint template is leaked for life, and you have ten
  • So: excellent as a convenient local factor beside a device you hold; poor as a remote credential on its own
  • This is exactly how phones use it — the fingerprint unlocks a key held in secure hardware; it is never sent anywhere

Attacking and Defending It

  • Presentation attack — a lifted print in gelatin, a printed photograph, a recorded voice
    • Answer: liveness detection — pulse, depth, involuntary movement
  • Template theft — the stored template is the irrevocable part
    • Answer: never store raw biometrics; keep the template in secure hardware, on the user's own device
  • Bias — error rates differ measurably across demographic groups. A single published accuracy figure hides that
  • And a legal dimension the other factors do not have: biometric data is special-category personal data nearly everywhere

Putting It Together

  • Authenticate with MFA, preferring phishing-resistant factors
  • Authorize with roles, default deny, least privilege
  • Store passwords with a slow, salted KDF, and check them against breach lists
  • Use biometrics locally, to unlock a key — never as the credential on the wire
  • Log every decision, including the denials, somewhere the subject cannot reach
